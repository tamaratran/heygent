  have stood on rocky headlands and lonely islands. They cast their beams
  across dark water so sailors could find their way home. Before satellites and
  radar, a single steady light could decide whether a ship reached the harbor or
  broke apart on a hidden reef.

  - The lens: The Fresnel lens, invented in 1822, used rings of prisms to bend
    scattered light into one powerful beam that could be seen for miles.
  - The keeper: Keepers trimmed wicks, wound clockwork, and polished glass
    through long nights, often with only their families for company.
  - The signature: Each lighthouse flashed its own pattern of light and dark, so
    a navigator could tell exactly which coast lay ahead.

  Most lighthouses today run on their own, and their keepers are long gone. Many
  towers have become museums, inns, or quiet landmarks that people visit on
  summer afternoons. Still, something about them stays with us. A lighthouse
  doesn't chase anyone or call out; it simply stays lit and trusts that whoever
  needs it will look up and see.
